# Case study 001 — Apple tree, fruit consumers, decomposers

## Question

Can location, timing, sequence, and concentration of resource access support fruit-feeding insects and decomposers **while preserving apple production and tree health across generations**?

## Producer and participants

Apple tree; fruit-feeding insects (distinguish species and life stages, including codling moth); fungi and bacteria (distinguish mutualists, saprotrophs, pathogens); other fruit consumers; soil biota; birds and other predators; future generations of all participants.

## Mechanisms to investigate

- Fruit feeding, marketability and biological edibility, premature drop, seed survival, pest life cycles, overwintering, reinfestation.
- Whether fruit on the orchard floor, dispersed elsewhere, or consumed in a different sequence changes pest survival and nutrient cycling.
- Beneficial versus pathogenic fungal species, infection routes, decomposition and soil conditions, including pH and oxygen; do not infer pathogenicity from fungal abundance alone.
- Producer health, crop yields, nutrient return, soil ecology, and seasonal feedback.
- Tradeoffs between insect reproduction, other organisms' nutrition, and long-run resource production.

## Hypothetical numbers, not measurements

The “2% eaten / 98% spoiled” apple is an **illustration** of how small consumption may reduce the resource's usefulness for another consumer. It is not a validated estimate of codling moth damage or ecological energy loss. Likewise, a mature tree producing hundreds of pounds of fruit does not mean all that fruit would be spoiled in one scenario.

## Candidate comparisons (not prescriptions)

1. Observed orchard conditions and life cycles.
2. Earlier access by different consumers, followed by decomposers.
3. Spatially separated insect substrates or alternative hosts, subject to species-specific reproductive feasibility.
4. Resource removal, composting, distribution, or timing interventions with complete ecological accounting.
5. No intervention as the baseline.

Do not presume “flies at the poo pile” is appropriate for **codling moths**, whose larvae have specific host requirements. Do not equate all flies, maggots, and moth larvae.

## Measurements

Fruit mass and quality; insect reproduction and survival; fungal community composition and pathogen incidence; tree growth and next-season yield; soil pH and nutrients; energy/material flows; other organisms' outcomes; delayed ecological effects.

## What would count as failure?

Any proposed arrangement that merely shifts pest pressure, starves another necessary organism, harms tree health, or relies on unsustainable external resources fails its stated whole-system objective. A failed treatment does not by itself refute the possibility of alternatives.

## Status

**Open research problem.** No experiment or complete lifecycle accounting has yet established a superior arrangement.
